# What we built, what it found, where it can go

## What we built

A system that maps fields of knowledge the way you'd map terrain — and
then looks at where the maps touch. Take two areas that never talk to
each other, map each one carefully without letting either side see the
other, then check: do they connect? Where exactly? What does one side
know that the other never says?

Every claim the system makes is labeled by how well it's proven, dead
ends are recorded permanently, and nothing is asserted without evidence.

## What it found

- **Prime-number research vs. the published math literature.** Mapped a
  year of independent research against the journals. They barely touch —
  except at one bridge: a 70-year-old formula both sides use. We then
  mapped that bridge in full detail.
- **The Riemann Hypothesis literature.** The three main approaches to
  the most famous unsolved problem in math barely touch each other.
- **The Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer literature.** Another famous unsolved
  problem: its two sides touch only through the exact bridge the
  problem itself is about.
- **Prime numbers, directly.** Compared the published literature against
  a working computer model of the prime sieve. It found four places
  where the model holds things the literature never states — all four
  verified by independent computer checks.
- **The history of AI itself.** Four rival schools of thought, mapped
  against each other. Same pattern: dense within, nearly disconnected
  between.
- **The stock market (running now).** Raw price data vs. company
  fundamentals vs. the news — mapped three ways to find where those
  stories actually connect.

## Where it can go

The pattern keeps repeating: knowledge is dense inside each field and
nearly disconnected between fields — and the breakthroughs sit on the
narrow bridges between them.

### Why money proves the idea

A dollar bill has no value in itself — it's paper. Its value is
entirely relational: what someone else will give you for it, the
network of people who accept it, the trust behind it. Every price is
a relation between two things, and the whole financial system is
relations built on relations — claims on claims on claims.

This system maps relations. It never asks what a thing is worth in
itself; it maps what things are worth *in relation to* each other.
That is why the market was its most natural test: we mapped raw
price data, company fundamentals, and the news against each other,
and found they interweave — value living in the connections between
them, exactly as the relational view predicts. The instrument maps
what value is made of.

The system doesn't care what the field is. It works anywhere knowledge
is split up: law, medicine, markets, chess, any science. Every map it
makes makes the next one more valuable, because each new area can be
checked against everything mapped before it. And it can be pointed at
any hard problem to show exactly where the difficulty lives — and the
narrow places where progress can cross.
